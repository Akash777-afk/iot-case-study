01 — Project overview

One place for NOC engineers to monitor smart meters, payment devices and connected cars.

The context

Airtel’s IoT Network Operations Center (NOC), through TierOne. Engineers look after devices for 12 enterprise customers in 7 cities. When devices stop reporting, they need to know why and who is affected.

Observed problem

Everything lived in one master file: 100,114 rows, 22 columns. Engineers filtered and pivoted it by hand to answer simple questions.

One faulty cell site showed up as hundreds of rows, and two similar status columns were easy to mix up.

Goals

  • Show overall device health on one screen.
  • Drill down from region to site to device, with affected customers.
  • Make bulk SIM Applet updates safe.
  • Keep a 100,000-row table fast.

My part

I studied the data, sketched the screens, designed the UI in Figma, and built and deployed the front end in React and TypeScript.

From sketch to screen

Mapping the ecosystem

Who sends data: smart meters, soundboxes, vehicle trackers and connected cars.

The signal path: device → SIM Applet → BTS → service provider.

Why it mattered: customer, SIM Applet and BTS became the core filters.

Two key flows

Diagnose an outage: spike on the dashboard → filter by circle → find the BTS → raise a ticket.

Onboard a device: check IMEI, ICCID and model → register → SIM Applet assigned → live.

What it changed: the dashboard links straight into Analytics and BTS details.

Bulk update flow

Find first: filter the SIM Applet list and select devices.

Validate first: errors send you back to fix fields, not to the start.

Review, then confirm: no mass update is one misclick away.

Flows into screens

Analytics: one filter bar drives a grid of downloadable chart cards.

Device details: SIM Applet beside network issues and KPIs.

Devices by circle: North, South, East, West, matching how NOC teams talk.

03 — The final solution

Four screens, four jobs

Screenshots from the deployed prototype. It runs on a static dataset and hasn’t been usability-tested yet.

Dashboard overview

Problem: no overall picture; counting problem devices meant filtering by hand.

Decision: summary cards up top, each opening its device list. Red, amber and green only for status.

Why: engineers see if anything needs attention before opening a table.

Device management

Problem: 100,114 spreadsheet rows were slow to scroll and filter.

Decision: a searchable table that only draws visible rows; CSV export keeps the filters.

Why: the exported file matches what the engineer was looking at.

Network analytics

Problem: linking failing devices to a cell site and its customers was manual work.

Decision: four filters drive nine charts; a region opens its cell sites and affected customers.

Why: it follows the order of an investigation: region, site, then devices.

SIM Applet management

Problem: 37,088 devices need an update; one wrong bulk update could hit thousands.

Decision: bulk updates capped at 1,000 per session, after confirmation.

Why: less repeated work, with a limit on how far a mistake can spread.

04 — What I learned

What I’d take forward

01Learn the domain first

Understanding circles and cell sites showed me engineers think by region, not by device.

02Big data is a design problem

At 100,000 rows, speed and export behaviour are part of the experience.

03Choosing what comes first is hard

Picking a few counts over 22 fields took the most rework. Customer info may deserve more space.

04Give status colours one job

Red, amber and green only for status keeps alerts clear. Still to test with users.

05Bulk actions need limits

Counts before confirming and a 1,000-device cap balance speed and risk.

06A prototype isn’t an outcome

A short path in a demo isn’t a faster investigation. Real proof needs real tasks.
